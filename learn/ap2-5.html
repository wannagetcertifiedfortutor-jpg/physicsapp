Tension and connected objects

Analyze objects connected by ideal strings; solve for tension and common acceleration.

Sample problem · AP Multiple Choice

In an Atwood machine with a light string and a massless, frictionless pulley, we take the tension to be the same on both sides of the pulley. Why?

  1. It is not: the tension is always larger on the side of the heavier block.
  2. Because the string's mass is negligible, any difference in tension would give it an enormous acceleration; so the tension must be the same at both ends.
  3. Because the pulley pushes on the string equally on both sides.
  4. Because tension is a property of the material and cannot vary along a string.
Sample problem · AP Free Response · Calculate

Two sleds on frictionless ice are connected by a light rope. The rear sled has mass 2 kg and the front sled 8 kg. A horizontal force of 20 N pulls the front sled forward. Find the acceleration of the sleds.

Pulley constraint conditions

Sample problem · AP Multiple Choice

Two blocks hang from the ends of a single string over a fixed pulley (an Atwood machine). Measure each block's position y1​, y2​ downward from the pulley axle. Which relation follows from the string having fixed length?

  1. a1​=2a2​
  2. a1​+a2​=g
  3. a1​=a2​
  4. a1​=−a2​

Tension in a massive rope

Apply Newton's law to a differential element to find T(x) along a rope with mass.

Sample problem · AP Multiple Choice

A heavy uniform rope hangs from a ceiling with nothing attached below. If it is slowly loaded by adding weight at the bottom until it breaks, where will it snap?

  1. In the middle.
  2. Anywhere — the tension is the same throughout.
  3. At the bottom, where the load is attached.
  4. At the top, where the tension is largest.
Sample problem · AP Free Response · Derive

A uniform rope of mass M and length L hangs vertically at rest from a ceiling. Let x be the distance measured downward from the top of the rope (0≤x≤L). Derive the tension T(x) in terms of M, g, L and x.
